Cavity Geometry as Computational Substrate:
Toward Shape-Encoded Photonic Reservoirs

Status: Public Domain Use: Open · No Permission Needed Domains: Photonics · RC Theory License: CC0
Motivation § 1

Reservoir computing (RC) is a recurrent neural network framework in which a fixed, high-dimensional dynamical system — the reservoir — transforms low-dimensional inputs into rich feature spaces that a simple linear readout can exploit. In physical RC, the reservoir is a material or optical system whose natural dynamics perform the computation. The central open question is: what physical properties of a reservoir determine its computational power?

Most photonic RC implementations to date use delay-line architectures — a single nonlinear node feeding back through a fiber loop with time-multiplexed virtual nodes. These systems are effective but geometrically trivial: the reservoir is a one-dimensional temporal sequence, not a true high-dimensional spatial network. Computational richness is borrowed from time, not from structure.

We propose that three-dimensional cavity geometry is itself a computational resource — that the shape of an optical enclosure encodes, via wave chaos and modal interference, a reservoir whose dimensionality is set by physics rather than by wiring.

Core Hypothesis § 2

A plano-hemispherical optical cavity — flat mirror base, curved high-index hemisphere — operating in a wave-chaotic regime supports simultaneously excited spatial modes whose interference patterns are dense, non-repeating, and sensitively dependent on input frequency and angle. This constitutes a natural reservoir state space.

The Berry-Robnik mixed phase space produced by deliberate dielectric scatterers within the cavity breaks integrable symmetry, suppresses mode clustering, and maximises the effective rank of the reservoir state matrix — the quantity that directly predicts computational capacity.

Readout is achieved via a conformal photonic skin layer: monolayer graphene patterned as a detector array on the outer surface of the hemisphere, coupled to the intracavity field through evanescent leakage. Each detector samples a spatially distinct superposition of cavity modes, producing the column vector Ij(ωk) that populates the reservoir state matrix Mtotal.

Graphene is chosen for its sub-nanometre thickness — minimising back-action on cavity modes — its broadband photoresponse at 1550 nm, gate-tunable complex refractive index (Xu et al., APL 2015), and demonstrated evanescent coupling responsivity. A single optimised waveguide-integrated detector has reached 230 V/W at zero bias (Scientific Reports, 2025); whether comparable per-channel performance survives in a dense conformal array is unresolved — see § 2.1.

Primary Risk · Array Readout SNR § 2.1

The dominant open risk is not the cavity physics but the readout. Monolayer graphene absorbs only ~2.3% of normally incident 1550 nm light; per-patch photocurrent is therefore intrinsically small, and recovering a clean signal vector from 20+ patches simultaneously must contend with thermal noise and inter-channel crosstalk. The single-detector responsivity figures reported in the literature do not transfer directly to a correlated array and should not be cited as if they did.

Worse, the two obvious mitigations are in tension. Increasing per-patch absorption — longer interaction length, cavity-field enhancement — tends to increase the spatial correlation between neighbouring patches, because adjacent detectors then sample overlapping evanescent fields. Strongly correlated channels are not linearly independent, and reff collapses even when every individual detector performs well. Raising raw signal and preserving rank therefore pull in opposite directions.

This is a tractable optimisation, not a wall, and it is resolvable in simulation before any fabrication. The achievable readout is the product of per-patch SNR and the number of mutually independent patches; the design problem is to maximise mutual information per channel subject to a correlation penalty, which fixes the optimal detector spacing and coverage fraction (sparse, optimised placement is expected to outperform dense tiling). If the modelled product cannot clear the reff > 50 threshold under realistic noise, the architecture does not beat delay-line RC baselines and should be abandoned — this is the controlling kill condition of § 4, H4.

One ordering caveat, surfaced by a toy numerical check of this section's logic: readout correlation is not the first bottleneck. The achievable reff is bounded first by the intrinsic spectral rank of the cavity field — how evenly mode energy is distributed across the resonances within the line-width — and only then by how the skin samples it. If the wave-chaotic field is dominated by a few high-Q modes, reff is capped at the source and no readout scheme can recover it. Spectral flatness is governed by cavity geometry and Q-factor, i.e. it is the H1 "shape → capacity" question. H1 and § 2.1 are therefore coupled and must be evaluated together, with H1 first: confirm the field is high-rank before optimising the patches that read it.

Preliminary Check · Toy Model § 2.2

The logic of § 2.1 was tested with a deliberately minimal numerical model (a one-dimensional rim-sampling caricature: a random superposition of spatial modes read by patches whose angular acceptance window widens with absorption). It is not a physical simulation and yields no real reff value; it exists only to check whether the stated tension is internally coherent and to reveal which variable dominates.

Two results. First, the tension is robust: across every spectral assumption tested, widening the patch window raised signal (mean SNR) while lowering effective rank — exactly the trade § 2.1 describes. Second, and more usefully, the dominant variable is the field's own spectral flatness, not the patch geometry. When mode energy was concentrated in a few modes, effective rank collapsed to ≈ 2–3 regardless of how the patches were arranged; when energy was spread evenly, patches recovered ≈ 90% of the channel ceiling. This confirms the ordering claim above: the H1 shape→capacity question is upstream of the § 2.1 readout question and must be settled first.

The model also surfaced a hidden-assumption error in its own first draft (an arbitrary spectral roll-off that pre-determined the rank), which is documented in the script rather than quietly fixed — a small illustration of why even toy checks earn their place. The code is released CC0 as a companion file; it requires only numpy and runs in minutes.

Open Research Questions § 4
  • Shape → capacity mapping. How does cavity geometry (aspect ratio, scatterer number and placement, hemisphere radius) quantitatively determine effective reservoir rank reff? Is there an analytic bound analogous to the Weyl law for eigenmode density?
  • Graphene skin optimisation. What detector spacing, coverage fraction, and gate-voltage profile maximises the mutual information between skin output and intracavity mode superposition? Does partial coverage outperform full tiling?
  • Frequency comb multiplexing. A frequency comb input (Nf teeth, ~0.5–1 GHz spacing) simultaneously probes Nf reservoir states per round trip. What is the information-theoretic limit of this spectral multiplexing, and how does it interact with cavity round-trip time?
  • Fabrication kill conditions. What are the explicit falsification thresholds — minimum reff, rim bond integrity, graphene mobility floor — below which the architecture does not outperform delay-line RC baselines?
Status & Use § 5

This is released to the commons under CC0. It is a seed, not a project: the architecture, the open questions, and the kill conditions are placed in the public domain so that anyone who finds them useful can build on them freely. No permission, attribution, coordination, or contact is required. There is nothing to join and no one to ask.

The most useful next step requires no laboratory: an FDTD model (MEEP or equivalent) of the geometry that computes the inter-patch correlation matrix and estimates achievable reff against the § 2.1 risk — establishing, in simulation, whether the readout can clear the reff > 50 threshold before any fabrication is attempted. That FDTD model does not yet exist and is the clearest open invitation here. The companion file that does exist is the § 2.2 toy check — a logic test, not a physical simulation. Take either, fork it, publish it, ignore the source — all are equally welcome. The only aim here is to move the question forward.
